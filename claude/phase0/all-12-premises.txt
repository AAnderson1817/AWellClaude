1 Chalk and Draft -- A dry limestone flue-system left behind by a drained sea, where blind mollusc-grazers the size of carts lick the walls away and every hole they leave changes which way the air moves through the whole system.
 A: A soft lump of chalk (contact-only marking)
   1st: Hold the lump against any surface you are touching — floor, wall, ceiling while hanging — and it lays a white line as you move. At 320x180 a single line crosses a third of a room, so you can scrawl across a whole wall in ten seconds, and the scrawl is still there when you come back four rooms later. It is a drawing toy: people will write on things before they have any idea what it is for. It has a finite charge and refills by rubbing against pale seam tiles, so 'find more chalk' is a self-explaining loop with no text.
   2nd: The chalk is not a marker, it is a scent. The grazers are eyeless and navigate by taste along stone; a fresh line will pull one across two rooms, and it takes three bites of the wall to get the last of the line off. The reinterpretation is that your idle doodle from twenty minutes ago is a demolition order with a delay fuse — and that you can therefore choose, precisely, which tiles in the world stop existing, by choosing where you scribble.
   impl: `u8 chalk[22][40]`: bits 0-3 = the four tile faces coated (L/R/U/D), bits 4-7 = accumulated bite-wear on that tile. Marking: while the button is held and the player hitbox overlaps a solid tile face, set that face bit and push {tileIdx, face, frame} into a 64-entry ring of LOOSE marks; a mark ages out of the ring after 90 frames and is then SET (permanent until eaten). Lump charge = `u8` 0..96, decremented per newly set face. Grazer pathing uses a BFS over the room's face-graph (~1200 faces max) producing `u8 distToChalk[22][40][4]`, rebuilt only on a chalk-dirty flag, not per frame. Grazer = `struct { i16 x,y; u8 face, dir, chew, shellChalk; }`, max 6 per room, advancing 1 px per 10 frames and at each tile corner choosing the adjacent face with the lower distToChalk. On a chalked face: chew++, at 48 the face bit clears and wear++; at wear==3 the tile is deleted and tilemapDirty is raised.
 B: A stiff sheet on a bone frame (a hand-held wing)
   1st: Hold it while airborne and your fall becomes a slow steerable drift; any moving air in the room visibly shoves you sideways, so falling becomes a thing you do on purpose. There is a one-per-40-frame flick that punches a short cone of air out in front of you and makes dust, hanging threads and your own hair move. It is the cheapest kind of fun: a stranger will jump off the highest thing in the room just to see what happens.
   2nd: Braced — feet planted, sheet held into the current instead of under you — it stops being a wing and becomes a valve. It deflects the local draft sideways for exactly as long as you hold it, so you can aim moving air at something on the far side of a room you are not touching and cannot reach. Combined with the fact that chalk laid in the last 90 frames is still loose powder, this converts a contact-only verb into a ranged one: you can paint a wall you will never stand on.
   impl: `i8 flowX[22][40], flowY[22][40]` derived from 24 Jacobi relaxation passes on a scalar potential seeded at fixed vent tiles and clamped at solids — recomputed ONLY when tilemapDirty is set (i.e. when a grazer eats a tile), never per frame. Wing held: gravity *= 0.32, xDrag = 0.88, vel += flow[tile] * 0.05. Flick: spawns into a fixed 128-entry `Powder` array {i16 x,y; i8 vx,vy; u8 life} any LOOSE chalk marks inside a 3-tile cone, clearing their face bits; powder advects by flow + 0.2*gravity each frame and, on striking a solid face, sets that face's chalk bit and dies. Valve mode: while braced, a 5-entry override list rotates flow in the 5 tiles downwind by the sheet's angle for as long as the button is held; released, the list empties — nothing persists.
 KEY: Tile deletion is the ONLY event that re-runs the airflow relaxation, and grazer chewing is the only thing that deletes tiles. So the two verbs form a closed loop with a delay in it: chalk a wall -> a grazer eats through it -> the new hole becomes an airway and the room's entire flow field changes -> the wing now has a draft where there was none -> flick loose chalk into that draft and the powder advects to a face you could never touch -> a grazer climbs to a place you cannot go and eats a hole there. Every room's flight envelope is a function of where you scribbled earlier, and you can bootstrap the whole chain from a single line drawn at floor level.
 HOOK: High in the wall of the first room, above the reachable ledges, sits a pale round plug of packed chalk the size of a doorway, set into darker stone. In minute one it is a stain: you have no chalk, no wing, and no way up. It is never highlighted. It becomes usable only when you know (a) that the vertical shaft in room one has no updraft because nothing has holed its floor yet, (b) that a grazer can be sent to hole that floor, and (c) that loose powder flicked into the resulting updraft will set on the plug — at which point a grazer climbs the shaft and eats the doorway open.
 INDIFF: The grazers have no eyes and have never once oriented toward the player. They read as five times the player's height and move at 6 px/s. To a grazer the player is a pebble: it detours around you with the exact same path curve it uses for a boulder, and if you are lying on a face it wants, the same stroke that takes the chalk takes you off the wall. Nothing in the world pursues, and there is no attack animation, because there is no attack — you can only ever be killed by standing inside an appetite that was never about you. The dread is that the thing which kills you will not have noticed.
 NOTREDUNDANT: Chalk writes persistent world state; the wing writes velocity. Chalk requires physical contact, changes nothing about how anything moves, and its effects survive you leaving the room — it is the only route by which tile matter changes at all. The wing touches nothing permanent: it cannot mark, cannot delete a tile, and cannot influence a grazer in any way (they are blind and deaf and read only surface taste), and every effect it has ends the instant the button is released. Neither can substitute for the other in any single-verb form; the only overlap is the deliberate composition where the wing transports powder the chalk has already produced, which requires both.

2 Sandcast Galleries -- A silt delta under a low stone canopy, where dog-sized mud-daubers have spent an age cementing every dry grain they find into hollow galleries, so the entire landscape is the crust of an older landscape that has already drained away.
 A: A leather bladder of dry silt (a pour you aim)
   1st: Squeeze it and a stream of dry grains pours out. It piles, slumps to its angle of repose, spills off ledges, runs downhill, pours through any one-tile gap and fills whatever it lands in. It is a falling-sand toy you can carry and aim, which is fun for its own sake for a very long time before anyone wonders what it is for; you will bury things and pour it off high ledges to watch the fan spread.
   2nd: Sand is not fill, it is a mould. The daubers crust only the exposed outside of a pile and never its base, and a crust cell is registered as solid tile while the sand behind it is not. So the pile is scaffolding for a shape you then remove: you are building with the cavity, and the material you poured is meant to leave. This inverts the obvious reading — the player spends the first hours making ramps and plugs out of sand and then discovers sand's actual job is to be a negative.
   impl: `u8 sand[22][40]` = grain count 0..8 per cell, stepped every 3rd frame in a bottom-up scan: a cell moves 1 grain into the cell below if that is <8, else diagonally into whichever of the two lower neighbours holds fewer (ties broken by a fixed x-parity so it stays deterministic), else rests. A separate `u8 crust` bitfield marks cemented cells; crust cells are solid for collision and are skipped by the automaton. Bladder = `u16 grains` 0..600, emitting 1 grain/frame at a nozzle offset. Wet cells (flagged by the room's water level) can hold grains but are never eligible for cementing. Dauber = `struct { i16 x,y; u8 state, targetIdx, timer; }`, max 3 per room, looping WANDER (walk the surface graph) -> if a dry sand cell with an exposed non-floor face lies within 6 tiles, APPROACH -> CEMENT (120 frames, sets that cell's crust bit) -> WANDER. The struct contains no reference to the player and the state machine never reads player position.
 B: A green reed pole about three tiles long
   1st: An eight-segment springy beam. Plant the butt, run, and it bends and flings you further and higher than a jump; stand on the end and bounce; wedge it and twang it. The bend is real and visible at 320x180, and it misbehaves — a bad plant angle drops you on your face. People will vault over things that do not need vaulting over.
   2nd: It is a spring, therefore it can be plucked. A planted pole, plucked, shakes the ground for eight tiles: dry sand liquefies and flows sideways like water, packed silt slumps, and any crust that loses the sand supporting it cracks and falls under its own weight. The pole's real function is not that it launches you — it is that it makes solid-looking ground temporarily stop being solid, including ground you are standing on.
   impl: Eight point masses with fixed rest-lengths and a bending stiffness term, integrated with Verlet at a fixed 1/120 substep; the butt segment pins to the contact tile when planted. Launch impulse = accumulated bend energy projected along the tip normal. Plant firmness is read from the contact cell: crust or bedrock gives full energy return (0.95), a sand cell absorbs it (0.15) and consumes 2 grains — so the crust rule teaches itself physically the first time you try to vault off a pile. Pluck: sets `u8 liquefy` = 90 on every sand cell within 8 tiles; while liquefy>0 the automaton lets a grain move into any neighbour whose count is lower-OR-EQUAL rather than strictly lower, which flattens piles and drains them through a single one-cell hole. Any crust cell whose 8-neighbourhood sand total falls below 2 is cleared and spawns three debris particles from a fixed 32-entry array.
 KEY: Daubers never cement the bottom row of a pile (the base cells have no exposed non-floor face), so every crusted pile is a shell with an open base. Plant the pole in the pile, pluck it, and the liquefy flag lets the interior grains walk sideways and pour out of that base seam in about two seconds, leaving a standing one-cell-thick cemented shell with a walkable hollow inside it and the sand recovered on the floor below for reuse. The same pluck run near an OLD gallery does the reverse: ancient crust that still has dry silt behind it loses its support and collapses, opening walls that read as permanent scenery. One button on one verb both creates rooms and destroys walls, depending entirely on whether the sand behind the crust is yours or the world's.
 HOOK: A grey cemented hopper protrudes from the ceiling of the first room, its spout sealed with a hardened plug — an obvious lump of mortar with an obvious funnel shape, on a ceiling you cannot reach in minute one. Nothing marks it. It becomes usable only once you know that a vault plants properly only in crust, that you can therefore pour and let daubers crust your own launch pad wherever you like, and that a pluck against a plugged crust with dry silt behind it blows the plug out — at which point an entire ancient column of black silt drains into room one, burying its floor and exposing the way down.
 INDIFF: A dauber responds to exactly one stimulus in the entire game: dry silica with an exposed face. It has no player-detection code at all. They walk across the player as terrain, stepping on you with the same gait they use on a rock. Stand under your own pour long enough and one will cement you in place — not to kill you, because it does not know you are there, but because you have become a surface. They are as tall as you and there are three of them and they will never once look at you. The galleries you are walking through are what happened to whatever was standing still here last.
 NOTREDUNDANT: Sand is matter without energy; the pole is energy without matter. Sand adds material that obeys gravity, sits inert forever once settled, and is the ONLY thing a dauber can perceive and the only route to new solid tiles. The pole creates nothing, carries nothing, cannot fill a gap, cannot plug a hole, cannot be left anywhere (dropped, it falls flat and supports nothing), and cannot make a dauber do anything, since daubers are insensible to vibration and motion. Conversely sand can never move the player, never launch anything, and never removes a tile. Neither verb can perform any action of the other; only the composition — pour, let it crust, then pluck — produces the hollow, and that requires both in sequence with a wait in between.

3 Dry Measure -- An abandoned chalk-and-lime working — soft white rock, settled dust on every ledge, still being slowly re-excavated by something blind that eats the walls.
 A: A surveyor's spring tape (curved steel ribbon on a reel, with a hook on the tip)
   1st: Hold the button and a curved steel ribbon shoots out along wherever you are pointing. It stands out rigid for a while, then wilts at one point and the rest of it hangs and swings from that fold. Release and it zips home with a rattle. A stranger in an empty room will spend a minute finding the exact length at each angle where it gives out, running that fold point deliberately along a wall like a finger, poking objects on the far side of the room, and letting the whole thing snap back for the noise.
   2nd: The tape's stiffness has nothing to do with how far it is out — it is decided by what its far end is touching. Get the tip onto solid tile and the fold disappears completely: the tape stops being a cantilever and becomes a strut braced between two surfaces, permanently rigid, strong enough to stand on and to carry a load. And the retract is a two-body problem obeying Newton's third law: it pulls whichever end has less friction. The same snap either drags a hooked object toward you, or drags you toward it, and you do not get to choose — the floor does.
   impl: Fixed array of 48 nodes at 2 px pitch (96 px max reach, under a third of a room). State: hand origin, aim angle (float), extended length L, hinge index h. Each frame, walk nodes outward from the root accumulating bending torque T_i = sum over j>i of m_j * |x_j - x_i|; the first i where T_i exceeds the section stiffness S becomes the hinge h. Nodes [0,h) are stamped onto the straight aim ray; nodes [h,48) are Verlet point masses with 2 px distance constraints and per-node tile AABB collision. Crucially: if any node in [0,h) is in contact with a solid tile, torque accumulation resets to zero at that node — which is exactly why a tape bearing on tile at both ends never folds at all, with no special case written for it. Grit resting on node i adds mass to m_i. Retract applies an impulse to whichever endpoint has the lower tile friction coefficient, and to neither if both are pinned (the tape just twangs).
 B: A pouch of quarry grit (fine dry limestone dust, refillable anywhere in the working)
   1st: Pour it and it heaps at its angle of repose, slumps, and avalanches down its own slope. Walk through a heap and you plough a channel through it that stays. Throw a handful up into a shaft of moving air and it hangs, drifts sideways, and settles somewhere you did not aim. Twenty seconds of just drawing lines on the floor and watching a pile find its own shape is already good.
   2nd: Grit is a friction dial, not a substance. A stone surface with dust on it is a slick surface — you skid on it, heavy objects slide on it, the grazer's feet lose purchase on it, and a single footfall wipes one unit of it away, so a dusted floor degrades under use. Second: because it is carried by air, a handful thrown into a room draws the room's invisible drafts in mid-air for two seconds, so grit is how you see wind. Third: grit in a bearing is a dry lubricant, which is the opposite of what grit on a floor appears to mean.
   impl: u8 amount per tile cell over the room's 40x22 grid (880 bytes, lives in the room arena, wiped on transition). Once every 4 frames, sweep bottom-up: if the cell below is neither full nor solid, move min(fall_rate, amount) down; else if the height difference with a diagonal neighbour exceeds the repose threshold (2 units per cell, about 34 degrees), move 1 unit sideways. A cell with amount >= 4 becomes collidable at its sub-tile fill height, so climbable slopes fall out of the automaton for free with no ramp authoring. Separate u8 'dusting' layer on solid tile faces sets that face's friction coefficient to 0.05 while dust sits on it, decremented by 1 per entity footfall. Airborne grit is 32 preallocated particles advected by the room's static per-tile wind vector field.
 KEY: One rule couples them and generates everything: the tape's retract accelerates whichever of its two ends has the lower friction coefficient, and grit is the only thing in the game that sets a friction coefficient. So the same action — hook a ledge, snap the reel — pulls the ledge's loose block to you if you dusted under the block, pulls YOU to the ledge if you dusted under your own feet, and does nothing but twang and shed dust if you greedily dusted both. It also fails in a legible, funny way: dust yourself, hook something heavier than you, and you are towed off a platform you meant to stay on. The second, slower coupling is load-induced folding — grit poured onto an extended tape adds mass at a chosen node, pushing the accumulated torque past S at exactly that node, so you can aim precisely where a rigid tape gives way and therefore where its load gets dumped.
 HOOK: In the first room a waist-high stone flywheel is half-sunk in the floor, its rim worn mirror-smooth, its spokes stopped mid-turn, its axle collar packed solid with set silt. Pushing it gives one pixel of wobble and a grinding note, and that is all it ever does for hours. Nothing in the room refers to it, nothing is drawn to point at it, no sound cue plays near it. It becomes usable only when you know two unadvertised things — that grit in a bearing is a lubricant, and that a load placed on an extended tape folds it exactly under the load — at which point you dust the collar free, brace the tape as a shelf across the shaft above it, and pour grit onto the shelf until it folds and drops the whole pile off-centre onto the rim. You cannot start it; you can only build something that drops onto it.
 INDIFF: The only large animal is blind and eats rock. It runs a fixed, slow circuit through the chalk, chewing new tunnels and collapsing old ones, and it navigates purely by vibration conducted through stone — so the only facts it holds about you are a snapped tape and a dropped heap. It never approaches you. It reorients its chewing, which sometimes means the room you entered by is no longer there. Nothing in the working wants anything from you; the working simply carries on being re-excavated with you inside it, and the dread is that you are a scheduling detail.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The tape acts only along a single line, only while held, only where you point, and cannot change any property of any surface. Grit has zero rigidity: it cannot span, reach, hold, or push, and every grain requires floor beneath it. The tape applies force at a distance; grit removes the ability of force to be applied at all. The one near-miss is that both can produce something you walk on, and that splits cleanly on support: a grit ramp needs continuous ground under every point of it and can only trade horizontal distance for height, while a braced tape needs solid tile at exactly two points and spans the empty space between them — the case where grit is useless. Conversely the tape can never coat, fill, reveal a draft, or make anything slippery, which is the entire second half of the game's vocabulary.

4 The Long Thaw -- A hillside ice-house cut into the black lake it was harvested from, kept frozen by its own depth and warmed by exactly one thing: an animal too large to fit inside a room.
 A: A felted wool blanket (heavy, oily, the size of the player and a half)
   1st: Drape it over anything and it takes that thing's silhouette and stays there. Flick a corner and a wave runs the length of it and cracks at the far edge. Drop off a ledge holding it overhead and you swing down slowly instead of falling. Drag it and it snags, folds, and drags a fold-shadow behind you. A stranger will cover a rock, pull it off, cover it again, and flick waves at nothing for a solid minute.
   2nd: The blanket is a stiffness state machine, not a piece of cloth. Dry it is full of air: drag, insulation, warmth. Wet it is heavy and clinging: no drag at all, it seals gaps, and it conducts cold instead of blocking it. Wet and cold it freezes into a rigid shell that keeps whatever shape it happened to be hanging in and will bear your weight until it thaws. The soft thing becomes furniture. Separately and never mentioned: wetness diffuses along the weave, so a blanket with one edge in water lifts water up over a lip and drips it out the low end for as long as you leave it — a wick is a pump you did not have to build.
   impl: 6x8 = 48 Verlet nodes at 6 px spacing (a 42x30 px blanket), structural + shear + bend distance constraints, 4 solver iterations per frame, per-node point-vs-tile collision. Two u8 fields per node: wet and temp. Aerodynamic drag per node scaled by (1 - wet/255) applied against node velocity along the local face normal, which makes a dry blanket a parachute and a soaked one a rag with no special-casing. Wetness diffuses one step along each constraint per 8 frames toward the drier neighbour; nodes overlapping a water tile clamp to 255; nodes in a warm tile lose wetness. A node with wet > 128 and temp < freeze_point sets FROZEN, which captures its current constraint rest-angles and switches that node from distance-only to distance+angle constraints; a contiguous run of >= 6 frozen nodes registers as a solid collidable span in the room's dynamic collider list.
 B: A block of lake ice (about 16x16 px, cut fresh from any of the working faces)
   1st: Too cold to hold barehanded for more than about three seconds before your grip fails and you drop it, so you push it instead — and it glides. Near-zero friction on stone: shove it once and it crosses the whole room, spinning slightly, clacking off the far wall and coming most of the way back. Stand on it and ride, and it slides out from under you the moment you lean. Nudge it into water and it bobs two thirds submerged. It is also visibly, continuously shrinking, which makes every idle minute with it feel spent.
   2nd: The block is not an object you own, it is water held in the wrong state and running out. Set it in a shallow stream and ice grows outward from it across the surface over about ten seconds, and you can walk across the thing you were carrying. Leave it in the sleeper's exhalation and it becomes a puddle, and that puddle is slippery, and later somewhere colder it is a floor. And it is transparent: things frozen inside a block are perfectly visible and completely unreachable, which is a fact about the whole world's walls and not just about the thing in your hands.
   impl: Fixed array of 8 ice entities per room in the room arena: { i16 x, y; i8 vx, vy; u8 w, h; u8 temp; u8 melt_accum; u8 flags }. Tile AABB sweep with friction coefficient 0.02 on bare stone, 0.30 on sawdust; buoyancy applied as an upward impulse proportional to submerged pixel area when overlapping water tiles, with a small restoring torque-free bob. Melt: melt_accum += ambient_temp[tile] each frame, and on overflow w and h each shrink by 1 px from the centre; ambient_temp is a u8 per tile diffused once per second from heat sources. Wrapping (a cloth node overlapping the block) divides the accumulation by 5. Impact above a velocity threshold splits the entity into 4 quarter blocks if free slots exist, each with the same volume total but four times the surface, hence four times the melt rate.
 KEY: Freezing the blanket is a graph traversal, not a state flip. An ice block touching any wet cloth node pulls that node's temp down; freezing then propagates outward through wet neighbours along the constraint graph at roughly one node every 0.4 seconds for as long as the block survives. Frozen nodes swap distance-only constraints for distance+rest-angle, so the blanket goes rigid in exactly the shape it was hanging in at that instant. Contact point and contact duration are therefore a continuous dial over how much of the cloth stiffens and where the boundary between rigid and floppy sits — pull the block away at two seconds and you have a stiff hook with a limp tail; at eight, a whole spanning arch. And it is self-cancelling in both directions: the blanket wrapped round a block cuts its melt to a fifth, but an insulated block freezes nothing, so preserving the ice and spending the ice are the same resource counted twice.
 HOOK: The first room's back wall is a cut ice face, and about a metre inside the clear ice, plainly visible and backlit green by the lake behind it, sits a dark object with a hand-shaped hollow worn into one side. It does not glow, pulse, or get a camera pause; the ice is simply a wall here and the object reads as something the quarrymen lost, i.e. scenery. It becomes reachable only with both verbs plus the unremarked observation that the sleeping animal's exhalation is warm and directional: soak the blanket, freeze it against a block into a rigid open trough, and that trough holds its duct shape for about a minute — long enough to carry one exhalation across a room and onto the face. Four breath cycles of redirected warm air opens it. You use the coldest thing in the world to build a machine that delivers heat.
 INDIFF: There is one large animal and you never see more than a piece of it: a flank filling the entire frame, a nostril the size of a door, a ribcage moving on a twelve-second cycle. It is asleep and it stays asleep. It is also the only warm thing in the biome, which makes it simultaneously the world's clock, its weather, and the thing quietly ruining everything you are carrying — and it is doing none of that at you. Standing in the breath does you no damage at all. It just melts your ice and dries your blanket, on its own schedule, forever, whether you are there or not.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The blanket has no mass, no temperature, and no motive power of its own; all it can do is change what other forces do — add drag, add friction, absorb, wick, insulate, cover, span. It can never move by itself, never reduce friction, never change the state of water. The block is nothing but mass and temperature: it slides, floats, bears weight, chills, and converts water between states, and it can never cover a shape (it is a convex rigid box), never absorb, never be anchored, never bridge a hole. The near-miss is that both can end up as something you stand on, and it splits on support and behaviour: the block must be held up from below by floor or water and will shoot out from under you the instant you push sideways; the frozen blanket is held at its edges, spans an opening nothing else in the game can span, and will not slide at all. Their timers even run opposite ways — the block is destroyed by warmth, the frozen blanket is destroyed by the same warmth arriving as thaw, so the one clock you cannot stop takes both, never at the same rate.

5 Chalk and Brine -- A drained inland sea: a shelf of porous white chalk under permanent overcast, where the only remaining water seeps from vents in the ceiling and every surface is either damp-dark, dry-pale, or sealed under glittering salt.
 A: The bladder (a gut squeeze-bulb)
   1st: A soft bulb you press against water to hear it slurp full (the sprite visibly swells over 64 units of fill), and squeeze to fire a ballistic jet of droplets. Tap for a fat lobbed blob, hold for a thin long-range stream. It is fun in the first ten seconds for the reason a hose is fun: you make arcs, you darken a wall in the shape you drew, you write on the floor with water and watch it fade. Pressed against a wet tile with no pressure it drinks instead, and you can suck a puddle dry one tile at a time.
   2nd: The jet has recoil. Squeezing downward while airborne pushes you up at 0.18 px/frame of added velocity, capped at 2.2 px/frame terminal — so a held squeeze is sustained flight for exactly as long as you have fill. The player stumbles into this by squeezing at the ground during a fall to see the splash. The consequence that makes it a real second reading: your movement resource IS your puzzle resource, and everything you fly over gets watered whether you wanted it to or not. Long flights leave a trail of saturation across a whole room's ceiling that will later slump, drip, and re-carve the room under you.
   impl: u8 bladder_fill (0..64). Jet = fixed array of 48 droplets {i16 x,y; i16 vx,vy; u8 alive} with gravity 0.25 px/frame^2; a droplet that hits a tile dies and adds +4 to that tile's saturation, clamped to cap[]. Suction drains 2 saturation/frame from the contacted tile into fill. The field itself is three parallel flat u8 arrays sized 22x40: wet[] (saturation 0..255), crust[] (salt thickness 0..8), cap[] (capacity, derived from tile type at room load). Field update is row-striped — 6 of 22 rows per frame, 4-frame period, ~240 cells/frame: each cell pushes min(wet, cap_below - wet_below) downward, then 1/4 of that rate sideways (capillary wicking), then loses 1 per 24 frames if it has an empty neighbour (evaporation). Crossing from wet>0 to wet==0 increments crust[] by 1 — salt left behind. crust>=4 makes the tile impermeable (cap=0). Chalk with wet>192 and nothing supporting it below converts to a mush tile that flows one tile/second and re-hardens on drying. Arrays live in the per-save arena, not the per-room arena: 25 rooms x 880 tiles x 3 bytes = 66 KB, so the world remembers. Rooms you are not in are caught up on entry by running up to 240 stored update steps at once.
 B: The plumb-stone (a dense carried rock)
   1st: A rock heavy enough to change how you move — carrying it lowers your jump, and you feel the encumbrance immediately. You lift it, you drop it, it rolls downhill, it plugs a seep, it sinks in standing water. When it lands, dust jumps off every ledge in the rock it landed on, in an expanding ring, and stops dead at cracks and gaps. That is the toy: whack the ground and read, from where the dust jumped, which stones are secretly one stone. Drop height sets the amplitude, so climbing higher makes a bigger ring — an analog dial the player discovers by climbing.
   2nd: The pulse only does work where the water already is. Saturated chalk under a pulse liquefies (thixotropy) and slumps in exactly the shape that was wet. And the ability you stumble into: strike above a pocket that is fully saturated AND capped by crust, and the water has nowhere to go — the crust bursts and the tile becomes a geyser for two seconds, which throws the player and the stone. Pressurised aquifers you made yourself, days of play earlier, without knowing.
   impl: One entity {i16 x,y,vy; u8 held, buried, sink_timer}. On landing, impact energy E = min(255, fall_px * 3). Pulse = one breadth-first flood over solid tiles from the impact tile using a u8 amp[22][40] scratch and a fixed 880-entry ring queue; amplitude a = E - 12*dist, terminating at a<=0, never crossing empty tiles, and fully absorbed by tiles with crust>=4 (salt deadens rock — this is what makes the Layer 2 pillar inert). One BFS per impact, never per frame. Then a single pass: amp>0 && wet>160 sets liquefy_timer=40 on that tile; amp>96 && crust>0 decrements crust by 1 and spawns a crack sprite; amp>0 && wet>200 && crust>=1 && all four neighbours impermeable converts the tile to a geyser emitter for 120 frames. The stone resting on a tile with wet>128 increments sink_timer; at 180 it is half-buried, and if that tile then dries (wet==0) it is cemented and immovable until re-wet above 128.
 KEY: Saturation is a shape and the pulse is a chisel that only cuts where the shape is. Spray a wall, and the droplets raise wet[] in precisely the tiles you hit; drop the stone, and the BFS liquefies only tiles with wet>160 — the wall collapses in the outline you painted, tile for tile. The turn comes from the crust rule feeding back into the BFS. Every failed spray evaporates and leaves crust[]+1, and crust>=4 absorbs the pulse entirely. So the rock is progressively insulated against your strikes by the residue of your own earlier wrong guesses, and a room you have been working in for ten minutes stops responding to the stone. The only cure is standing water deep enough and long enough to dissolve crust — which requires damming a basin, which requires a slump, which requires a strike. You must spend the water you can no longer make land.
 HOOK: In the first room, a floor-to-ceiling column of salt crust, thick and glittering, with a dark articulated shape suspended motionless inside it. It is plainly there and plainly solid. Spraying it does nothing: the jet sheets off impermeable crust and runs down. Dropping the stone against it does nothing: crust>=4 absorbs the pulse, so not even dust jumps. It is only ever dissolved by standing water held against it for a sustained period, which means flooding the first room, which means damming its outflow lip, which means knowing that a wetted chalk ridge slumps under a high-drop strike, and that a slumped ridge re-hardens into a dam when it dries. Nothing labels it, nothing greys out, nothing counts.
 INDIFF: The Wader: a many-legged thing as tall as the room that walks the basin drinking saturation out of the chalk through its feet. It has no interest in the player at all and no attack. Where it has passed, wet[] is zeroed and crust[] is raised — it is a slow patrolling eraser of everything you have plumbed, and it does not know it is erasing anything. If the player is standing in a puddle it drinks, they are tumbled by the suction, incidentally, the way a leaf is. The dread is that you have spent twenty minutes routing water and a thing that has never looked at you is going to walk through it because that is where it drinks. Scale mismatch and disinterest, no aggression, and nothing you can do about it except route around a creature that will never notice you did.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The bladder is the only thing in the world that can raise water — against gravity, across a gap, into a tile that was dry. It cannot put a single unit of energy into rock: crust, geometry, and structure are completely deaf to it. The plumb-stone is the only thing that can put energy into rock, and it cannot create, destroy, or relocate one unit of saturation on its own; it can only release potential the water already has. Its one fluid effect is bounded to prevent overlap: dropping it in a pool sloshes water within a contiguous pool only, never uphill and never into a tile more than one step from the pool's own edge. Neither substitutes: the bladder alone can wet a whole room and change nothing structural forever; the stone alone in a bone-dry room only makes dust and noise for eternity.

6 The Cold Comb -- The interior of an abandoned comb of grey wax, sealed inside a cold rock shell, where a few fixed shafts of daylight fall through cracks at one angle and the architecture itself is a material that runs when it is warm.
 A: The sun-bead (a heavy glass marble)
   1st: A fist-sized glass ball you carry, roll, and drop. It makes no light of its own. When it sits inside one of the room's light shafts it throws a hard bright focal dot onto the first surface behind it, and steering that dot by walking is immediately, stupidly satisfying — the dot slides across the wax, the wax under it goes glossy, sags, and runs. You can sign your name on a wall in drooping wax before anyone tells you what the thing is for. Out of the light it is just a heavy marble that rolls downhill and clacks.
   2nd: It is also a mass, and it heats itself. Left lying in a shaft on a wax floor, the bead's own temperature climbs, the wax beneath softens, and it sinks under its own weight — boring a smooth vertical shaft down through floor after floor, unattended, while the player is in another room. It works when you are not holding it. The corollary you learn the hard way: a bead that bores into shadow stops and cools, and is now a glass marble at the bottom of a sealed hole you cannot see, and getting it back is a problem in heat delivery, not in reach.
   impl: Entity {i16 x,y,vx,vy; i16 temp}. The room has one global light vector (fixed, e.g. 1 right : 2 down) and a precomputed u8 lit[22][40] bitfield from the shaft mouths, recomputed only when wax[] changes occlusion. If the bead's tile is lit, march the light vector from the bead to the first tile with wax>=32; that tile gets temp += 3 per frame, and bead.temp += 1 per frame. Unlit, bead.temp -= 1 per 16 frames toward ambient. Sinking: if bead.temp > MELT and the tile below has wax>0 and its temp > MELT, move the bead down 1 px and subtract 2 wax from that tile. The field is i8 temp[22][40] (ambient -20, MELT 32) plus u8 wax[22][40] (fill 0..64; collidable at wax>=32 && temp<MELT). Temp update is row-striped over 4 frames: t += (tUp*2 + tDown + tLeft + tRight - 5t) >> k, with the double weight upward for convection and k selected per material (wax k=4, wick k=1, glass k=6, rock k=5), then a pull toward ambient for tiles touching void. Wax flow, swept bottom-to-top so it is order-deterministic: temp>MELT && wax>0 && wax_below<64 moves min(4,wax) down every 8 frames, carrying temp-4 with it. Arrays are per-save, 25 rooms x 880 x 2 bytes = 44 KB, so melt is permanent and heat leaks between rooms through the shared edge column, caught up on entry.
 B: The wick (a length of dry pith cord)
   1st: A limp cord you lay, drape, drag, and pull taut. It hangs off ledges, catches on tile corners, and can be plucked like a string — it hums, and the hum is a real physical event in the room. It produces no heat whatsoever; in a cold room it is a piece of string, and a stranger will still spend a minute arranging it across the geometry because draping a rope over things is fun. Where it is touching something hot, it goes hot along its whole length in about a second and a half — six tiles a second — which is far faster than wax conducts.
   2nd: It has capillary action. A wick with one end in molten wax drinks the wax and carries it ALONG THE CORD, including uphill, depositing it at whichever node is coolest. This means the cord drains a molten pool upward, and the wax that sets on the cool section makes the cord rigid and load-bearing. You have accidentally built a beam out of a floor. The second half of the second reading is the inverse, which players find by ruining their own work: a wick with one end dangling into the cold shell is a heat DRAIN, and a cord you left lying there will quietly freeze the chamber you were trying to melt.
   impl: 24-node verlet chain {i16 x,y,px,py; i8 temp; u8 waxload; u8 rigid} with the usual corner-catch: maintain an array of tile-corner pivot points the cord has wrapped, pivoting about the last corner caught. At rest, nodes snap to tile centres so the chain maps cleanly onto the grid. Conduction along the chain each frame: t[i] += (t[i-1] + t[i+1] - 2*t[i]) >> 1, and each node exchanges with its tile at shift 3. Capillary: a node whose tile has wax>0 && temp>MELT pulls 1 wax/frame into waxload while waxload<8; each frame waxload migrates one unit from node i to whichever of i-1/i+1 has lower temp. A node with waxload==8 && temp<MELT sets rigid=1: its distance constraint stiffens to near-inextensible and it gains a 4x4 px collision box, so a set section is a standable rail. Re-heating a rigid node above MELT clears rigid and dumps its waxload into the tile below.
 KEY: Lay the cord with one node in the bead's focal tile and the far end coiled inside a dark sealed cell. Heat runs the cord at six tiles per second into a place light physically cannot reach, and the cell melts from the inside out. Then the same cord runs backwards: the melt it just made is drunk up by capillary and migrates along the cord toward the cool nodes, so the chamber empties itself uphill through the very cord that opened it, and the cord thickens and sets into a rigid rod you can now walk on into the space you just drained. One object, two flows, opposite directions, simultaneously — you set it up expecting one of them.
 HOOK: In the ceiling of the first room, among a hundred identical hexagonal capped cells, one cell is bulged, and the thing inside it shifts when a loud thing happens nearby. Obviously there, obviously sealed, never labelled. It resists everything you can do in the first two minutes for structural reasons, not arbitrary ones: the bead is a mass that only ever sinks, so it can never deliver heat upward; and a wick is limp and hangs, so it cannot be laid to a ceiling. Reaching it requires the knowledge that a wax-soaked wick sets rigid, that a rigid section can be re-heated at its far end to add another soft segment, and that a cord can therefore be built upward in stages against gravity — with heat fed from a bead sitting in a shaft on the floor, four rooms of melting away.
 INDIFF: The Combers: pale larval things much larger than the player that crawl the comb extruding fresh wax, repairing every hole they encounter. They have no eyes and no interest. They will wall the player into a chamber as routine housekeeping and continue past. They are also warm bodies — a mobile heat source you can tap with a wick, and a mobile undoer of your excavation, and they are the same animal. Nothing in their behaviour changes when the player is present or absent, which is the whole feeling: you are not being hunted, you are being tidied around. Being sealed in is never a softlock, because a Comber is itself hot enough to melt what it just built if you lay a cord to it.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The bead is the only source of heat in the game, and it is constrained twice: it works only where the fixed light shafts already fall, and as a sinking mass it only ever moves material DOWNWARD (melt, flow, set lower). The wick creates nothing — zero degrees, ever — and in a cold room it is inert string; but it is the only thing that moves heat, and the only thing that moves wax UPWARD, and the only thing that reaches sealed and lightless volumes at all. The bead cannot reach around a corner or into the dark; the wick cannot warm anything unless the bead already did. The bead can never lift a gram of wax against gravity; the wick can never melt the gram it needs to lift. Neither is a worse version of the other in any situation.

7 Salt and Afterglow -- A disused mineral works cut into terraces, lit only by damp mats of glowing moss that grow wherever water seeps, and grazed by blind animals the size of the room that scrape the walls for salt.
 A: The afterglow slate — a palm-sized pane of phosphor-coated slag on a wire handle, held up in front of you
   1st: Anything bright that passes behind the slate leaves a glowing smear on its face that fades over about five seconds. So you wave it through a drip of luminous seep-water and it keeps the streak; you swing it in a circle and hold a glowing loop in your hand; you walk past a moss patch and the slate keeps a bar of it. It is a five-second-memory etch-a-sketch made of light, and a stranger will spend two minutes scribbling with it before wondering what it is for. Critically it emits nothing into the room: it is a receiver, a screen, never a lamp. It cannot brighten one pixel of the world.
   2nd: Two things you stumble into. (1) Because it integrates over time, it turns rhythm into geometry: a drip every 1.4 s becomes a dotted line whose spacing you can literally read off, a rotating thing becomes a closed ring, and two events that look identical resolve as visibly different smears when both are in frame at once. (2) The ability: when a cell of the slate saturates, the slate goes opaque-bright there, and an opaque slate is a hand-held eclipse. The room's posterised lighting normalises to the brightest thing currently visible; hold the saturated slate over the one blinding seep-vent and it is excluded from both the display and the exposure maximum, so the entire rest of the screen lifts and dim detail resolves. You discover the tool's real use is subtraction — you make the world dimmer in order to see more. Nobody expects a light-tool that works by covering things up.
   impl: `u8 glow[24][16]` charge grid on a single `Slate` struct (`i16 x,y; u8 held; u8 glow[24][16]` = 390 bytes, session arena). Per frame, for each cell, sample the composited emissive value at the corresponding world pixel: `g = min(255, g + (src>>2))`, then `g -= DECAY(1)`. Rendered as three posterised levels through the existing hash dither. Saturation: cells at 255 set an `opaque` bit; the render pass builds an axis-aligned mask rect from the opaque cells and (a) skips those pixels when computing the frame's luminance maximum for the threshold pass, (b) draws flat slate-white over them. Zero writes to `tiles[22][40]`. Zero heap.
 B: The salt gourd — a pierced dry gourd you tip to pour a stream of coarse mineral salt
   1st: A falling-sand automaton in your hands. Salt pours, bounces, piles at a fixed angle of repose, flows around ledges, pours through a one-tile gap into the space below, and a settled pile is solid enough to stand on and walk up. Pouring granular material into a shaped hole is a toy older than games; you will fill a crevice for no reason at all and enjoy it.
   2nd: Salt is hygroscopic and this world's light is wet. A settled grain sitting on a moss tile drains it: a moisture counter ticks down and after about two seconds the moss goes dark and hard, permanently. The physical verb's real second reading is that it *destroys light* — the tool that adds matter is the tool that subtracts illumination, and it does so irreversibly unless water reaches the tile again. You find this by accidentally burying a moss patch you were standing on and watching the room lose a quarter of its brightness while you are still in it.
   impl: `u8 fill[22][40]` (0–8 subcells of settled salt, room arena, wiped on transition) plus `Grain grains[64]` in flight (`i16 x,y; i8 vx,vy` = 6 bytes each, fixed-point 8.8). Settle rule per grain: if the cell below has fill<8 move down; else if a diagonal-below cell has fill ≤ this cell's fill −2, slide there; else deposit (`fill[y][x]++`, free the grain). Angle of repose emerges from the −2 threshold, no trig. Salt participates in collision by promoting a cell to SOLID once fill≥6. Moss drying: `u8 wet[22][40]`; each frame `if (fill[y][x] && tileFlags[t]&EMISSIVE) if(--wet[y][x]==0) tiles[y][x] = TILE_MOSS_DEAD;`
 KEY: Grains in flight are the only fast bright objects in the world — they catch the rim light — and the slate integrates them. So a poured stream *draws on the slate*, and the shape it draws is the grain trajectory: a free pour is a bar, a pour that clips a slanted ledge is a bent parabola, a pour split by a stalactite is a fork, and grains that land on your own head and slide off draw the outline of your shoulders. Verb B is the pen, verb A is the paper, and the room's geometry is the hand holding it. The second-order coupling is the one that matters: burying moss with salt lowers the room's ambient light, which lowers what the slate integrates, which makes a faint smear that was previously drowned out finally readable. You use the destructive verb as the exposure control for the perceptual one.
 HOOK: A shallow stone catchment basin set flush into the floor of the very first room, about eight tiles wide, its bottom a dull grey-green identical in colour to the slate you are about to find, with a small rusted iron weight sitting in the middle of it. It is unmissable, it is walked across in the first thirty seconds, and it does nothing — no glow, no sound, no collision quirk. It is a slate the size of the floor that has been integrating this room's light continuously since long before you arrived, saturated flat and read by the player as ordinary stone. It becomes usable only when you know both (a) that the phosphor decays when starved of light and (b) that salt permanently kills moss — and only when you have found the route to the ceiling seeps of that first room, which is knowledge from the far side of the map. Bury every moss tile in room (0,0) and the basin's charge starts falling; because the phosphor under and around the iron weight aged differently to the rest, the decay is uneven, and for about ten seconds a figure resolves in the floor that nobody drew, and then it is gone. Never labelled, never counted, no residue in any UI.
 INDIFF: The salt-lickers are blind grazers taller than the room's visible ceiling, of which you usually see only a flank and a tongue entering from one edge. They have no model of you whatsoever: they will walk through your position and simply displace you along the floor, no damage, no reaction, no acknowledgement — the horror is that being shoved aside by something enormous produces no event at all. The only thing they respond to is salt, so pouring a staircase near one means it turns, licks, and eats your staircase while you are standing on it. It is not attacking you; it does not know you are up there. Scale mismatch and appetite, nothing else.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The slate is strictly read-only with respect to world state: its entire output is a charge grid drawn on its own face and a mask rect excluded from the player's display and exposure maximum. It cannot move, block, extinguish, create, or convert a single tile or grain. The salt has no memory and no display: it cannot show a past state, cannot hold an image beyond the physical persistence of a pile, and cannot make anything legible except by physically removing the emitter. The apparent overlap is deliberate and is the L12 trap — both can make a dim thing readable — but they are ontologically opposite. The slate changes what reaches your eye; the salt changes what leaves the wall. A moss-eating grub on the far side of the slate stays fed and lit; a moss-eating grub on a salted tile starves. That discrepancy is what teaches the player they did not understand the tool they had been using for four hours.

8 The Small Far Thing -- Terraced clay drying-floors on a hillside under a permanently luminous overcast sky, where the only creatures small enough to fit inside a single screen are the sifters that crawl the racks, and everything else is a piece of darkness moving slowly across the glow behind the scaffolding.
 A: The drop-lens — a water-clear bead the size of a thumbnail hung in a wire bail, held near the eye or out at arm's length
   1st: The world is drawn in two depth bands: the near band you stand in, and the far band behind it (sky, distant scaffolding, whatever is crossing the glow). Held close, the lens magnifies the far band ×2 about the bead — the dither pattern of a distant wall resolves into an actual texture, and a thing crawling on the far scaffold becomes a creature with legs. Held at arm's length, past focus, the far band flips and shrinks to ×½ — a shape bigger than the room becomes a small upside-down thing sitting in your palm. Sweeping between the two hold states passes through a smear. Everyone who has ever held a lens does exactly this for two minutes with no goal, and the joy here is specifically the joy of making something enormous small and upside down.
   2nd: It is not only a thing you look through — it is a thing that throws. Held in the arm's-length state with a bright far-band region on one side and a near solid surface within about three tiles on the other, it forms a real inverted live image on that surface, written into the actual light buffer, not into your eye. So you can take a shape that is enormous and unreachable and put a moving picture of it, mouse-sized, on the wall next to your hand. You stumble into it by accident while playing with focus and finding that a small upside-down picture of the sky has appeared on the rock behind you. And the near band's inhabitants — sifters, which orient to any small bright drifting thing because they eat glow-flies — cannot tell that picture from prey, and will crawl to it, and are heavy enough to matter.
   impl: Requires one premise-driven extension to `world.c`: a second flat background array `u8 far[22][40]` beside the existing `u8 tiles[22][40]`, drawn to its own 320×180 index buffer before the near pass — the same shape as Animal Well's `tiles[2][22][40]`. `struct Lens { i16 x,y; u8 state; }`, one instance. Viewing: for pixels inside a radius-9 circle centred on the bead, resample the far index buffer at `p' = c + ((p - c) * num >> shift)` with `(num,shift) = (1,1)` for ×2 and `(-4,3)` for ×(−½). Integer only, no float, no shader. Projecting: when `state == FAR`, raycast up to 24 px along the bead's facing axis in the near tile array for a SOLID face; if found, blit a 12×12 downsampled, 180°-rotated copy of the far buffer's above-threshold pixels into `light[180][320]` at that spot, clipped by BLOCKS_L. Sifter AI reads that same light buffer for its bright-drifting-thing target, so it is fooled by construction rather than by a special case.
 B: The daub — a fistful of grey slip-clay from a pouch that refills at any wet trough, thrown underhand
   1st: It splats and sticks wherever it lands, covering about two by two tiles, and then it sags: the anchor stays put while the mass droops a pixel a second, stretching into a teardrop, until at around six seconds it either necks off and the lower lump falls free, or — if it landed on a dry tile — stiffens and stops, becoming permanent solid clay you can stand on. Throwing mud at a wall and watching it drool is a pleasure that needs no explanation, and timing the drip so you can catch the falling neck-off lump on a ledge below is a game you invent for yourself in the first room.
   2nd: Two daubs whose sagging bodies touch fuse into one, and a fused pair anchored at two separate points stops sagging and pulls taut instead, snapping into a thin straight solid thread between the anchors. You find this by daubing either side of a gap for no particular reason and watching the two blobs reach for each other and become a bridge. Nothing announced it; it falls straight out of 'blobs merge' plus 'merged blobs share tension'. The corollary you find later is that clay is opaque, so a daub thrown into the far band is a hole punched in the sky, and it is a hole whose shape keeps changing for six seconds.
   impl: `Daub daubs[24]` in the room arena: `{ i16 ax,ay; i16 cx,cy; u8 age; u8 state; i8 link; }` = 12 bytes each. Per tick: `if (state==WET) cy++;` clamp the anchor–centroid separation; stamp collision cells into an overlay so the sag is solid. Merge: O(24²) AABB test on stamps — if two overlap and their anchors differ, set mutual `link`, and thereafter render and collide both as a 1-px straight line interpolated anchor-to-anchor instead of a teardrop. Bake: `if (age > DRY && !(tileFlags[tiles[ay][ax]] & WATER)) { tiles[..] = TILE_CLAY; free slot; }` — permanence goes into the flat tile array, not into an entity. A throw with 'up' held targets the `far[22][40]` array instead of the near one, which is how you get clay into the sky.
 KEY: Clay is the only opaque thing you can put into the far band, and the lens is the only thing that can bring the far band back as a small near object. Throw a daub across the glowing sky and you have authored a silhouette at enormous scale and unreachable distance — and because the daub sags, that silhouette is a shape that morphs over six seconds. Hold the lens up and it throws a live inverted picture of your own drooping clay onto the wall by your knee, at the size of a beetle, moving. B authors a shape that is huge, far, and changing; A returns it small, near, upside down, and in the light buffer where the sifters read it. Neither half is any use without the other: a daub on the sky is decoration, and a lens with nothing shaped behind it projects a featureless smudge.
 HOOK: In the first room, a tall wooden drying rack stands empty against the wall with a chipped pane of ground glass mounted in a swivel frame at the top of it, tilted at a useless angle, holding nothing, catching no light. It is obviously a made object, obviously old, obviously doing nothing, and it is the first thing in the game you will try to interact with and get nothing from. Behind it, about thirty pixels back, is the only completely blank whitewashed panel in the world. The rack's lower arm is weighted — it swivels when something heavy enough lands on it, which nothing in your possession is. It becomes usable only at the very end, and only with all three pieces at once: that a daub thrown upward sticks to the far band and sags, that the lens throws a real image onto a near surface, and that a sifter lured onto the lower arm by a projection is exactly heavy enough to swing the ground glass into line. Then the pane, the projection and the whitewash resolve something that has plainly been waiting to be resolved for a very long time. No label, no counter, no marker on any map.
 INDIFF: You never see one of the large animals. You see a region of the glowing sky stop glowing, slowly, entering from the left edge, and then leaving. That is the whole of the encounter: the locked 320×180 frame is physically incapable of containing one of them, so scale is enforced by the camera rather than asserted. While a shadow crosses, the ambient drops and your projections simply stop forming for about eleven seconds — the animal has not noticed you, has not aimed at you, and has taken your only tool away as an accident of its passing. The sifters, the one creature you can see whole, have no model of you either: they will walk into a wall repeatedly trying to reach a picture of a light, and will walk over your hands to do it.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The lens moves zero mass. Its only effect on world state is a small rect of the light buffer containing light that already existed and has been relocated and rescaled; it cannot stick to anything, cannot occlude anything, cannot support weight, cannot bake a tile. The daub cannot change the apparent size, depth or position of anything — whatever it does, it does exactly and only where it physically is, and it can only go where you can throw it. The categorical line: the lens relocates and rescales an appearance but cannot originate a shape; the daub originates a shape but cannot move its appearance one pixel from where the matter sits. The lens can put an image somewhere you can never reach; the daub can put matter only somewhere you can already reach. Neither can be substituted for the other in any room, and the projected-image lure is unavailable to the daub because clay is not bright and the sifters do not track dark things.

9 Brine and Ballast -- A drained salt-works of terraced evaporation pans, roofed by collapsing timber scaffold, where blind filter-feeders the size of barges plough slow circuits through the deep brine and everything the sea left behind is still crystallising.
 A: The Kettles — a shoulder yoke carrying two sealed iron kettles you fill with brine at a pan rim, or empty over it
   1st: Your own weight becomes a dial you turn. Empty, you moon-jump: high, slow, drifting, comically unable to shove anything. Full, you plummet — a four-tile drop lands with a thud that dimples the salt crust and throws a puff of dust. A stranger with no goal spends a minute just filling and emptying at a rim to feel the jump arc stretch and squash, and another minute dropping from the scaffold at load 4 to hear the different thud each floor material makes.
   2nd: Weight does not scale your movement — it swaps which floors exist. The salt crust over the shallow pans holds a body of load 0–1 and shatters under load 2+, so the light player has a whole extra walkable plane the heavy player has never seen. Conversely the kettles are sealed air vessels: empty, they float you on the brine surface and you physically cannot descend; full, you sink and walk the pan floor, a second map underneath the first. The player stumbles into this by fleeing something onto the crust while light and later returning heavy and falling straight through the ground they remember as solid. Every room is two rooms and the seam is your own body.
   impl: `u8 load` 0..4 on the Player struct. Five-entry const lookup tables indexed by load, in the same px/frame units as the existing tuning: `JUMP_V[5]`, `GRAV[5]`, `TERM_V[5]`, `SINK[5]` (signed — negative at load 0/1, so brine buoyancy is the same line of code as brine sinking). A `u8 loadMap[RH][RW]` in the room arena is cleared each frame; the player stamps `1+load` into the one or two tiles under its feet, and resting props stamp their own constant. New tile kind `T_CRUST` (TF_SOLID|TF_CONTIG) carries a per-tile `u8 stress[RH][RW]` in the room arena that increments while `loadMap >= 2` and swaps the tile to `T_EMPTY` at 24; because the room arena is wiped on transition, the crust recrystallises when you come back, with no save state. Filling is a hold on a tile flagged `TF_RIM`, one kettle per 20 frames, animated by the kettle sprite filling — no counter, no icon.
 B: The Gaff — a short iron salt-hauling hook on a haft, swung by hand onto a corner within arm's reach
   1st: You bite a corner and become a pendulum. There is no aiming, no throwing, no return: the hook catches an outside corner inside a 24px arc of your hands or it clacks off. Once caught you swing, and holding up or down slides your grip along the haft — pull in and you whip faster, pay out and you slow and lengthen. Nothing about this needs a goal; letting go at the top of an arc and watching the launch angle come out of where you released is the whole toy.
   2nd: The pivot is a two-body constraint, not a fixed point, and the player discovers this by accident when they hook something that isn't a wall. Hook a drifting salt raft or the dorsal plate of one of the filter-feeders and the constraint solves the other way: the anchor is towed toward you, or — if the anchor outweighs you — you are towed by it, hanging off an animal that never registers the contact and carries you the length of a room it was going to cross anyway. And the corner itself is a body: heavy swings shear salt corners off in half an arc, so the same input is a swing when you are light and a demolition when you are full. You learn that the world's furniture has a weight class and you are near the bottom of it.
   impl: At room load, one pass over `tiles[RH][RW]` builds `Corner corners[192]` in the room arena: `{ i16 x,y; u8 quadrant; u8 worn; i16 anchorEnt; }` for every convex solid corner, plus entries appended by moving entities with `anchorEnt >= 0`. Catch = nearest corner within 24px whose quadrant faces the player's approach. Pivot state on the Player is five floats: `ax, ay` (re-read from the anchor entity each frame if `anchorEnt >= 0`), `r` clamped 12..28, `theta`, `omega`. Integration is one line, `omega -= GRAV[load] * sinf(theta) / r`, with angular momentum conserved on grip changes by `omega *= (rOld*rOld)/(r*r)`. The two-way part is a single ratio: `mu = (1.0f+load) / ((1.0f+load) + anchorMass)`, and the constraint correction is split `mu` to the anchor, `1-mu` to the player; static tile corners use `anchorMass = 1e9f` so `mu` is 0 and the general case degenerates to a pure pendulum for free. Release velocity is the tangent `omega*r` plus the anchor's own velocity. Corner wear: `corners[i].worn += (1+load)` every 8 pivot frames, and at 32 the nub tile is swapped out and the gaff drops you mid-arc.
 KEY: The `mu` ratio in the gaff's constraint reads the kettles' `load`, so one hook has three completely different outcomes on the same object depending on what you weigh. Hook a floating salt raft (anchorMass 3) at load 0: mu = 0.25, the raft barely moves and tows you across the pan. Hook the same raft at load 4: mu = 0.625, the raft is dragged down and under, the constraint pulls it beneath the brine line, and you are left treading in the hole where your transport used to be. At load 2 the raft tips and pours its cargo. Nothing in either verb was written to produce that — it falls out of one mass ratio being shared between a physics constraint and a jump table, and it means the correct weight for a hook is a thing you can only learn by being wrong about it.
 HOOK: A cast-iron diving bell, man-height, sitting mouth-down on the crust at the lip of the first room's pan, a rusted lifting eye on its crown and a drift of shrimp shells banked against its rim. In Layer 1 it cannot be pushed, entered, climbed, or tipped, and no room is built around it; it is the thing you walk past. It becomes usable only once you know the crust load rule, own the gaff, and have failed at the deep pan — because the deep pan is the one place the two verbs contradict each other: you must be at load 4 to sink to the floor, and you cannot come back up from the floor at load 4, and there is no rim down there to empty at. Hooking the bell's eye at load 4 and hanging puts a combined 8 on the crust beneath it; the crust gives, and the bell goes down with you clinging to it, carrying its trapped air pocket. Inside the pocket there is a rim. You go down heavy and come back light. Nothing says any of this.
 INDIFF: The filter-feeders neither pursue you nor avoid you. Each ploughs a fixed circuit through the deep pans, mouth open, straining brine; standing in the circuit means being pushed under the crust by a body that never changed course, and the only evidence you were there at all is that nothing changed. The gaff makes this worse rather than better: you can ride one, and it will carry you exactly where it was already going and scrape you off under a low arch it has been passing for years, at the same speed, with the same rhythm. The dread is that the largest thing in the world has no opinion about you, and that the second-largest thing — the crust — will drop you the instant you weigh what an adult weighs.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The kettles change scalar magnitudes along the gravity axis only: jump impulse, gravity, terminal velocity, sink rate, and the number stamped into loadMap. They cannot convert vertical motion into horizontal, cannot hold you against gravity, cannot attach you to anything, and cannot move an object that isn't underfoot. The gaff adds no energy and alters no property of your body: it is a positional constraint that redirects momentum you already had. It cannot make you fall slower, cannot raise your apex above the anchor you are hanging from, cannot crack crust, cannot sink you in brine, cannot press a load plate. The one place they look like they overlap — 'both of them get you up' — is where they are most cleanly split: load 0 raises your jump by two tiles and cannot cross a gap at all, while the gaff crosses gaps and can never lift you higher than the corner you caught.

10 Vault of Husks -- The hollow interior of something enormous and long dead — a chalk-white vault of paper combs and root-sieves where slow moth-things groom the debris of their own shed lives into chutes, and the wind is one indifferent creature's breathing.
 A: The Elytra — a scavenged pair of stiff wing-cases worn on the back, held open with a button
   1st: Held open in the air you get a real airfoil: lift scales with your horizontal speed, so you cannot climb from a standstill but you can dive to build speed and flare to trade it back for height. Porpoising down a long vault — dive, flare, dive, flare, skimming a hand's width above a floor you never touch — is the entire pleasure, and it needs no goal whatsoever. Open them standing still and they buzz and shed a haze of scale-dust that hangs in the air and drifts, which is beautiful for its own sake.
   2nd: Open, you are a sail before you are a wing. The vault has air in it that the player initially reads as decoration, and the elytra turn you into the thing that reads it: stand on a ledge with them open in a crosswind and you are pushed along it without walking; open them in the wake of one of the big groomers and its bow-wave throws you across a gap you were not attempting. The dust the wings shed is the tell — it makes the invisible field visible, so the player who opened them pointlessly in room one has already been shown where the room's currents are and will only understand it later. Above a certain airspeed the wind stops having authority over you at all, which is the moment you notice it ever did.
   impl: `int elytra` on the Player, forced false while curled. While open and airborne, three lines on the existing `vx, vy` floats: lift `vy -= LIFT_K * fabsf(vx)` capped at the current gravity, horizontal drag `vx -= DRAG_H * vx * fabsf(vx)`, vertical drag at roughly triple that so the fall rate collapses. Wind is a static per-room field authored as a second tile layer and unpacked into `i8 windX[RH][RW], windY[RH][RW]` in the room arena (mostly zero; only vent rooms are populated), added to velocity only when `elytra` is set — so posture, not position, decides whether wind exists. Moving creatures stamp transient gusts into a fixed ring of 32 `{ f32 x,y,vx,vy; u8 ttl; }` — no fluid solver, 32 impulses with a falloff radius. Scale-dust is 64 preallocated particles that sample the same two arrays, so the visualisation and the physics cannot disagree.
 B: The Curl — you tuck into a hard sphere, like a pillbug pulling shut
   1st: Curled you are round: you roll, you keep almost all of your speed through collisions, slopes accelerate you, and you have no steering at all. Finding a terraced slope and curling at the top to see how far the accumulated speed carries you across the room is a toy a stranger understands in one press. Losing control is the fun part — you commit, and then you watch.
   2nd: Curled, the world stops classifying you as a body and starts classifying you as debris — because that is literally what the code checks. The vault is full of machinery and animals whose whole job is moving fallen matter: sweeper roots that brush past an upright figure and push a curled one, sorting chutes that reject a standing shape and swallow a round one, a groomer moth that will not touch you until you are curled on its back and then flicks you off with the rest of the husks. You travel by being garbage. The first time a chute you have walked past twenty times swallows you and delivers you to a room you had no route to, the discovery is not that you gained a power — it is that the vault was always sorting, and you finally matched the filter.
   impl: `int curled` on the Player. Hitbox swaps from the upright 8x12 to an 8x8 that fits a one-tile gap. Collision response changes from the existing clamp-to-zero to `vn = -vn * 0.80f` on the contact normal and `vt *= 0.98f` tangentially, so bounces are elastic and rolls are near-lossless; slope tiles add a constant `vx += SLOPE_G * dir`; horizontal input authority is simply skipped while curled, and the only input read is the uncurl. The whole second reading rides on one byte, `u8 playerForm` = `FORM_UPRIGHT` or `FORM_DEBRIS`, which sweeper roots, chute mouths, sieve grates and the groomer's brush branch on with a single comparison — the same byte the husk props already carry, so the entities were never written to know about the player at all.
 KEY: The elytra are physically on your back, so curling shuts them: `elytra &= !curled`, one line, and the two verbs become mutually exclusive states with all the play in the transition frame. Momentum carries across the swap but the force set is swapped with it. Rolling loses almost nothing tangentially and gains on slopes, and lift scales with `fabsf(vx)`, so opening at a lip converts accumulated ground speed directly into altitude; curling at apex zeroes lift and drag at once and you keep the whole `vx` as a ballistic pellet that fits a one-tile gap and bounces at 0.8. The surprise nobody designed: elastic bounces plus slope gain means speed ratchets across a room instead of decaying, and past roughly 3 px/frame the wind term is small enough relative to `vx` that opening the wings in a vent no longer takes you where the vent wants — you outrun the room's grip on you, in a game where being gripped by rooms is the normal condition.
 HOOK: A paper comb the size of a house hanging from a single fibre in the ceiling of the first room, far above every ledge, with one smooth round aperture in its underside about the diameter of a curled body. It is obviously there, obviously scenery, obviously inert; nothing in Layer 1 refers to it, no ledge is placed to suggest it, and the fibre is out of reach of any jump or glide from anywhere in the room. It requires the curl (nothing else fits the aperture), the elytra (nothing else reaches the height), and specifically the knowledge that a roll can be ratcheted into a launch — you have to arrive there fast, open at the lip, climb, and curl at apex so that the pellet you have become goes through a hole rather than the body you are bounces off it. The round hole is the only teaching: once the player owns a round shape, a round hole stops being decoration, and that recognition happens hours after they first saw it.
 INDIFF: Nothing in the vault hunts. The moth-things groom, the roots sweep, the sieves sort — the whole ecology is housekeeping performed by animals and mechanisms far too large to have noticed that one piece of the debris is alive. A groomer will brush you into a chute with the same stroke it uses on shed scales and never break rhythm; the wind that pins you against a grate is something's breathing at a scale where your presence is not a rounding error but simply not a term in the equation. The horror is the classification: you have to make yourself into refuse to travel, and the vault is completely willing to treat you as refuse, and it will never once check.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The elytra apply only off the ground, add force, give lateral authority, and increase the world's aerodynamic grip on you; they cannot bounce you, cannot shrink you through a one-tile gap, cannot preserve momentum through an impact, and cannot change what the sorting machinery thinks you are. The curl applies only on surfaces, adds no force except slope gravity, removes all authority, and shrinks your silhouette and your classification byte; it cannot generate lift, cannot slow a fall, cannot be steered, cannot resist a wind field — indeed it makes wind stop existing for you entirely. They cannot even be held at once, because a curled body has its wing-cases shut. Where they might have overlapped — 'both are ways of crossing a gap' — the split is total: the elytra cross a gap by adding altitude you did not have and can be steered mid-flight; the curl crosses a gap by spending speed you already earned on a trajectory you cannot alter after the first frame.

11 The Proving Floor -- A bakehouse the size of a cathedral that nobody has staffed in a very long time — still warm, still running, its lower halls silted knee-deep in old flour.
 A: A lump of live leaven (sourdough starter you carry in a cloth)
   1st: You set it down on any surface and it swells. It is soft-bodied: you sink into it, it takes your shape, it squeezes through a gap and comes out the other side elongated, it lifts anything light resting on top of it, and it plugs a hole airtight. A stranger will spend two minutes just putting it in places and watching it inflate, and will laugh the first time they sit on one and slowly rise. It grows faster where the floor is warm and stops dead where it is cold, which people work out in seconds without being told.
   2nd: It has a clock. Left too long in heat it over-proofs: the blob collapses into slack, and slack dough is not a solid any more — it is a falling-sand fluid that seeks the lowest point, pours through gratings the intact lump could never fit through, and re-thickens into a solid lump once it reaches somewhere cold. So the same object is a jack, a plug, a platform, a timer, and — if you deliberately cook it too long — a liquid you can decant into a place you cannot reach. Second: because it seals airtight, a lump grown into a vent traps whatever the vent is pushing, and the pressure has to go somewhere else.
   impl: `u8 dough[88*48]` on a 4px subgrid (80x44 in use): 0 = empty, 1..200 = proof level, 201..255 = slack. `u8 heat[40*22]` sampled from tile flags. Growth pass at 20 Hz, double-buffered, fixed scan order: a cell with proof>=64 and heat>0 spends 32 proof to claim a free 4-neighbour at proof 32. Lifetime proof over 255 flips a cell to slack; slack cells run a falling-sand rule (down, then down-left/down-right, parity alternating per frame for determinism). Blob identity from a flood-fill label pass, max 4 blobs of 512 cells. Anchoring: a cell touching a solid tile with `dust==0` is anchored; thrust = sum of the inward normals of all anchored cells, and when the anchored count hits 0 the whole blob's centroid translates at 8 px/s along the residual.
 B: A tin flour dredger (a perforated shaker of grey flour)
   1st: You sift and a slow cloud comes off around you, drifts on whatever air the room has, and settles into drifts on ledges. It has no weight and no collision — it cannot hold anything up. It is pure pleasure to hold: you shake it and the room's invisible air becomes legible, curling around a doorway, pouring off a shelf, spiralling above a warm tile. Kick a drift and it puffs. Walk through one and you leave prints.
   2nd: Flour is a recording medium, and the thing it records is not yours. Dust a hall, leave, come back: there are tracks across it that are not your size and not your gait, going somewhere. Dust a smooth blank floor and the shape of something standing on it appears as an absence. And the real reinterpretation is inverted — because dough will not anchor to a floured surface, the useful tool is often the *removal* of flour, and the game never says so. Flour also coats: a floured creature is visibly clumsy for a while, and floured dough is a lump you can no longer stick anywhere.
   impl: `u8 dust[40*22]` depth 0..4, drawn as a 4-step ordered-dither ramp, plus `u8 tread[40*22]` — an 8-column bitmask per tile recording scuffing, which is what makes footprints readable at 8px. Airborne: `struct Mote { i16 x,y; i8 vx,vy; u8 life; }` in a fixed array of 256 with an index free-list. Per-room static `i8 wind[20*11*2]` (one vector per 2x2 tile block) authored in the room record; motes integrate wind + 0.25 gravity + a repulsion term from any entity moving faster than 1 px/frame, so an expanding dough blob visibly shoves the cloud. On rest, dust[tile]++ (clamped at 4). Any entity's feet on dust>0 decrement it and stamp the tread mask; a sliding dough blob sets dust=0 on every tile it crosses.
 KEY: Dough anchors only to tiles where `dust == 0`, and expansion pushes off its anchor set. Flour the floor under a lump and it loses its anchors, so symmetric growth against a wall becomes net horizontal thrust and the blob slides along the floured strip — and because a sliding blob wipes `dust` to 0 behind it, it re-anchors the instant it runs off the end. The length of the floured strip is exactly the distance the lump travels, which is a rule a player can measure with a shaker and then aim.
 HOOK: A long shallow stone trough runs along the base of the wall in the first room, filled flush with a grey, rock-hard set mass — a sponge left to die decades ago. You walk over it for the entire game and it does nothing; tapping, standing on, and growing dough beside it all produce nothing. It is not dead, only dry, cold and — by the end — buried under the flour the player themselves has been dusting into that room since minute one. It wakes only when live leaven makes bare contact along its length, which requires knowing that live dough propagates into set dough, and that flour is the thing preventing it: the one action that satisfies both is sliding a floured lump the length of the trough, because the slide wipes the trough clean and delivers live dough in the same motion. Nothing is ever labelled, and no room is built to suggest it.
 INDIFF: Nothing in the bakehouse is hunting. The machinery simply keeps handling material and the player is material: millstones turn, a conveyor of trays goes into a dark aperture and comes back empty, and all of it will process a body exactly as it processes a lump, at a scale where you are the crumb. The mill-moths are dog-sized and want spilled flour; they walk over you to get to it and their feet are cold. The dread beat is that a lump you sent into the aperture comes back baked into a shape you did not make, and the machine does not pause to show you.
 NOTREDUNDANT: Flour has zero volume and zero collision — it can never support weight, block a gap, lift, plug, exert force, or occupy space; it is a state written onto surfaces and air. Dough is opaque and mute — it never reveals what is under, behind, or moving through a room, records nothing, cannot be applied to a surface without anchoring to it, and cannot be made to hang in air. Every use of flour is about adhesion, visibility and memory; every use of dough is about volume, force and time. Neither can substitute for the other in a single case.

12 The Sounding Works -- A dead ore-dressing works of iron gantries and stone hoppers hung across a chasm, where the machinery still runs by itself and everything alive down there is blind and listens through the metal.
 A: A steel tuning fork with a sliding rider weight on one prong
   1st: Strike it against your palm and press the stem to anything; the vibration goes into the material and you watch it travel. The whole works becomes an instrument — iron rings bright and far, stone drags and dies, timber thuds, packed rust eats the tone instantly. Sliding the little rider weight up and down the prong changes the pitch, and the rider is drawn as a visible pixel, so nobody needs telling. The first fun thing anyone finds is dust: any grit sitting on a plate you are driving crawls into standing-wave patterns and slides along them, and you can walk a loose pebble across a plate you never touch by choosing where to press and what pitch to hold.
   2nd: The ring does not stop at what you can see. It travels through whatever is acoustically continuous with the surface under the stem, so it maps the insides of things: a wall that rings hollow, two structures that look separate but answer as one piece, a girder whose ripple stops dead three tiles in because there is a crack there you cannot see. And the thing you stumble into is that you are not the only listener — the blind things in the works feel vibration through the same metal, so the tool that reads the world also writes your position into it, and the two facts arrive several rooms apart.
   impl: `i16 amp[40*22][2]` (cur/prev), fixed-point. Per frame: `next = 2*cur - prev + k[mat]*(sum of bonded 4-neighbours - 4*cur)`, then `next -= next>>6` for damping. `k[mat]` comes from a material index in the tile flags (iron fast and low-loss, stone slow and lossy, timber mid, rust ≈ 0). Two tiles are bonded when `bodyId` matches and the shared edge carries no `crack` flag, or when the active cramp edge links them. Driver: while the stem contacts tile t, `amp[t] = A * sin_table[(f*t_frames) & 255]`, with A decaying to zero over 4 seconds from the strike and f fetched from `freq[riderPos]`, riderPos 0..7. Loose entities standing on a tile take `vel += -grad(|amp|) * s`, which makes them migrate to nodal lines for free; tiles whose |amp| passes a threshold shed their attached debris.
 B: A joiner's sash cramp (a single iron clamp with a screw)
   1st: You set the jaws across two things and turn the screw, and from then on those two things are one object. Clamp yourself a handhold onto a passing ore-car and ride it. Clamp a swinging counterweight to the wall and the swing stops. Clamp a hopper door open. It is one physical cramp, not an ability: if you clamp it to something that leaves, it leaves with the cramp, and you have to go and find it. Turning a screw and feeling two loose things become one rigid assembly is satisfying before it is useful.
   2nd: Joining two driven bodies does not average them — it stalls them, and the machine does not know it has stalled: it keeps pulling, strain builds in the cramp, and after a while the cramp shears off and is flung a few tiles away. So a stalled machine is a temporary stationary platform with a countdown you can hear in the pitch of the strain. The reinterpretation you fall into is that a cramp does not only join two objects; it can close the two lips of a single fracture, which is not joining anything at all — it is repairing continuity inside one body, and everything that continuity carries starts moving again.
   impl: `u16 bodyId[40*22]` static per room, up to 32 movable bodies as `struct Body { i16 x,y,vx,vy; u8 driver; u16 tileStart,tileCount; }`. One cramp: `struct Cramp { u8 active; u16 tileA,tileB; i16 strain; }`; placement requires the two target tiles to be within one tile of each other and either belong to different bodies or be the two sides of one crack edge. Placement or removal rebuilds a union-find `u8 parent[32]` over bodies; a merged set holding two conflicting drivers zeroes all velocities in the set and accumulates `strain += |sum of driver forces|` per frame, and at strain > 4096 the cramp shears, is thrown 2-3 tiles, and lies on the floor until retrieved. The same cramp edge is appended to the acoustic bond table the wave stencil consults, which is the entire coupling between the two verbs.
 KEY: The cramp writes one extra edge into the acoustic bond table that the wave stencil reads, so it makes two separate solids — or the two lips of one crack — into a single ringing body. A tone you can only inject on this side of a gap crosses it; a nodal pattern you mapped on one plate redraws itself across both plates and the grit on the far plate migrates to lines you set from here; and a girder whose ripple used to stop dead at a fracture now carries the ring all the way through, which is also how the blind things now know there is something down the far end.
 HOOK: Lying half-sunk in the floor of the first room is a cast counterweight the size of a house with a hairline fracture running across its face. It is the first thing you walk over and you keep walking over it for the whole game. Tap it early and it gives a dull thud, which is exactly the sound the player files under 'dead scenery', so the game has already answered the question and closed it. It is never labelled, never lit, never framed by a room. It becomes usable only when you have mapped the crack's path by tapping, know that a cramp restores continuity across a fracture rather than merely joining two objects, and can find the single point where the fracture's lips sit close enough for the jaws. Then the dead lump rings, for the first time, and it is the loudest thing in the world.
 INDIFF: The blind things navigate by vibration through the metal and come to any sustained ring because ringing is what feeding used to sound like; they will pass within a tile of a player standing on stone and never register them, and they will walk straight over one standing on iron without any change in gait. Nothing pursues and nothing is angry. The ore-cars run their loop with or without a body on the rail, the gantries take up their loads on schedule, and the works is simply much larger than the player and busy with something that stopped mattering a long time ago.
 NOTREDUNDANT: The fork adds no matter and applies no force to any attached body — it cannot move, hold, join, separate, or support anything; it only drives a scalar field and shifts grains that are already loose. The cramp has no readout of any kind — it cannot tell you what is inside a wall, whether two structures are one piece, where a fracture runs, or that a hollow exists; its reach is one tile and it changes exactly one rigidity edge at a time. The fork is a pure instrument that changes what you know; the cramp is a pure fastener that changes what is rigid. Neither can be used to do the other's job in a single case, which is why the whole design hangs on their one point of contact.
